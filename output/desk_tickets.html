What carried between tickets

Running the three tickets in order left state behind that later tickets picked up, which is the whole reason the board is worth watching in sequence:

  • Invoice 501 opened the gate for 103. Ticket 101 paid Bulldog Print Co, so by the time ticket 103 asked the same vendor for 12 hoodies, Inventory's get_open_invoices_for_vendor check came back clear and the five-day lead time actually held. Run 103 first and the same agent correctly refuses to promise a date.
  • The balance is read live, never cached. Accounting on ticket 102 quoted $2,560.00, not the $3,400.00 it would have seen at the start, because ticket 101's payment had already landed.
  • Stock counts did not move. Nothing shipped during this run, so inventory is unchanged. The tee reprint and the hoodie top-up are both still inbound.

1. How would you evaluate the performance of the agents on each ticket, and why?

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee

I predicted that the boss would call inventory first because we have an out of stock item, and then delegation would go to accounting for the overdue invoice, because we have a payment and billing issue, and then there would be a customer service message drafted saying the tee would be back in stock but there are none currently, and that we are getting the tees reprinted. The agent matched my prediction, and all of the tools I predicted fired for that specific agent.

The boss routes the stock issue to inventory first. Inventory checks to see if we have it on hand, finds that we are one short, and then recognizes that the vendor is holding an unpaid invoice. So it routes the problem to accounting, and accounting checks the balance, sees that the invoice is overdue to Bulldog Print, and asks for human verification. Lastly there is a customer service email drafted to say that it is out of stock but we will confirm a date of arrival before charging. This one also requires human approval. So I would say the agent team on 101 did a really good job.

Ticket 102 — Rent due

I predicted that the boss would call facilities first, because they are the ones in charge of the shop, and that includes rents and leases, and we are talking about Elm City Properties here. Then I thought we would delegate to accounting because we are looking at a cash outflow. This was an exact match on routing and on tools with what the model actually did, so I would say the agents did a fairly good job with this one as well.

When I ran the agent team, the boss noticed facilities was aligned with the shop space, so it moved us to facilities and then funneled it to accounting because of the rent bill. The boss closed out the loop after accounting required human approval. A box actually appears at the bottom of my screen that says needs your approval, so I am notified and I can approve the payment, and it says only I can release the cash. So it does a good job making sure the human stays in the loop.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount

I predicted that the boss would call inventory first to check for feasibility and would see the gap of 12 hoodies. Then, since this involves looking at margins and restocks, it would delegate to accounting and to customer service to send a message to the customer saying we are waiting on goods to be shipped.

103 matched my routing when I ran it. It went to inventory, then accounting, then customer service. But on tools, get_open_invoices_for_vendor never actually fired. That was a gate I had to manually go back and have Claude fix. It matters, because the 12 hoodies come from Bulldog Print Co — the same vendor sitting on the unpaid invoice from 101 — so without that check the agent was willing to promise a five-day lead time it could not actually honor.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

Sometimes all the routing can get confusing, and it would be easier if I could click on each specific agent to see its processes. Right now I am mostly getting a summary — route ticket for boss, feasibility check for inventory — which is readable, but it can be a little overwhelming or confusing, especially as this scales.

Beyond that, a single agent with many tools would have been simpler for a shop this size for a few concrete reasons:

  • The routing is fixed anyway. A rent notice always goes to facilities then accounting. A stock issue always starts at inventory. When the routing never really varies, five agents are five chances to hand off wrong for a decision that was never in doubt.
  • Every handoff loses context. Inventory already knows the vendor is blocked by an unpaid invoice, but it has to re-explain that to accounting in a sentence instead of just calling get_open_invoices_for_vendor itself. One agent holding the whole ticket never has to re-state what it already knows.
  • Nine tools is not a lot. The split exists to keep each prompt small, but one agent can comfortably hold all nine of ours. The specialization is buying us less than it costs at this scale.
  • It would be cheaper and faster. Five prompts per ticket instead of one is five times the tokens for a ticket whose whole resolution is three database reads and a payment.

Where the team does earn its keep is the audit trail. Because each step is attributed to a named agent, I can see exactly who checked what and which tool backed it up — and for anything touching cash, that traceability is worth the overhead. So my honest read is that the split is overkill for routing but genuinely useful for accountability.

4. Three new problems Campus Customs might face that this agent team could solve with the tools we built

A. Quoting a brand new product

Say someone wants to carry a new crewneck. Boss routes it to inventory, which uses get_vendors to find which supplier actually does apparel reprints and what their lead time is, and get_open_invoices_for_vendor to confirm that supplier is not blocked. Accounting then uses get_pricing on our comparable products to see what the shop already charges and what margin it holds, and get_cash_balance to check whether we can even fund an opening order. Customer service drafts the quote. Everything needed for this already exists; nothing new has to be built.

B. A closure or supply disruption

There is a huge snowstorm and the shop is closed for the day, or it is near Christmas and a supplier gets wiped out by a blizzard, so orders go on backlog. Boss routes it to inventory, which uses get_tickets to pull every open order and get_inventory_stock to see what is actually sitting on the shelf versus what was promised, and get_vendors to see whose lead time just became fiction. Customer service then drafts the notice for the mailing list, and because drafts stay internal on the board, a human reads it before anything goes out. The same shape covers a holiday promotional email, where accounting runs get_pricing first to find a discount that still clears our margin floor before customer service names a number.

C. Which products are actually selling

We want to know which products move fastest, and in which sizes and colors. Inventory uses get_inventory_stock across every SKU and size to find where we are at zero and where we are overstocked — right now that would flag the tee in size S and the crest mug both at zero, while size L sits at 14. Accounting pairs that with get_pricing to show which of those SKUs are worth restocking first by margin, and get_cash_balance to say what we can afford this week. The output is a restock priority list that accounting can hand to inventory, which also tells us how fast the reshipments actually need to come in.

5. Three new problems this agent team could NOT solve with the tools we built

A. Scheduling and trading employee shifts

We have no system set up for this at all. There is no staff table, no availability, no shift calendar — the database knows about products, vendors, leases and cash, and nothing about people. What it would need: a staff and shifts schema, tools like get_shift_schedule, get_staff_availability, propose_shift_swap and confirm_shift_swap, and a new Scheduling agent that owns coverage rules — minimum staff per shift, who is trained on the register — with the same human approval gate we use for cash, since a swap that leaves the shop uncovered is its own kind of expensive.

B. Talking to suppliers, or finding new ones

We currently cannot communicate with suppliers directly, and we cannot discover new ones. Our vendors table has exactly three rows that someone typed in, and the shop rules explicitly say do not call vendors — every draft stays internal on the board. So the idea in question 4A, where inventory and accounting work out what they want made and send it to a producer, stops right at the point of actually sending it. What it would need: an outbound channel — a send_vendor_rfq tool and a real email or portal integration — plus a search_supplier_directory tool hitting an external source, and a Procurement agent that can run a quote comparison across replies. It also needs a human approval gate on anything outbound, for the same reason payments have one.

C. Returns, refunds, and anything that puts money back

Our whole cash model only runs one direction. The shop rules say cash only flows out, there is no revenue path in the database, and log_payment and /approve-payment only ever subtract from checking. So the moment a customer returns a hoodie or disputes a charge, the team has nowhere to put it — there is no returns table, no way to reverse a payment, and no record that a sale ever brought money in. What it would need: a sales and returns schema so a sale exists as a record in the first place, tools like log_sale, log_refund and get_sales_history, a cash model that moves both directions, and a Returns agent working with accounting on restocking fees and refund eligibility — again behind human approval, because a refund is a payment.

Two alternates for C, if you would rather swap it: (1) Confirming a delivery actually arrived. The board can mark invoice 501 paid and say the reprint is coming in five days, but nothing in the system ever learns whether the box showed up. It would need a receiving table, a log_receipt tool and carrier tracking. (2) Reconciling what we forecast against what we sold. Accounting can compute that a hoodie at $49.30 returns 55.4% margin, but it can never check whether the Yale AI Club actually bought them, because no sale is ever recorded.
