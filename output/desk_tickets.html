What carried between tickets

Running the three tickets in order left state behind that later tickets picked up, which is the whole reason the board is worth watching in sequence:

  • Invoice 501 opened the gate for 103. Ticket 101 paid Bulldog Print Co, so by the time ticket 103 asked the same vendor for 12 hoodies, Inventory's get_open_invoices_for_vendor check came back clear and the five-day lead time actually held. Run 103 first and the same agent correctly refuses to promise a date.
  • The balance is read live, never cached. Accounting on ticket 102 quoted $2,560.00, not the $3,400.00 it would have seen at the start, because ticket 101's payment had already landed.
  • Stock counts did not move. Nothing shipped during this run, so inventory is unchanged. The tee reprint and the hoodie top-up are both still inbound.

Five agents, all running live on gpt-6-luna through Pydantic AI, every fact pulled from the database over MCP. Because they are real agents and not a script, two runs of the same ticket do not look identical — so where the run diverged from what I predicted, I have said so and worked out whether the choice was defensible.

Question 1 — How would you evaluate the performance of the agents on each ticket, and why?

I want to answer this agent by agent rather than ticket by ticket, because once they could all talk to each other the interesting thing stopped being "did the ticket close" and started being "did this specific agent do its own job properly." All three tickets closed. That is the low bar. The real question is whether each agent went and checked before it said anything.

Boss — strong, once I forced it to read first

The Boss is the one agent I had to constrain structurally. In the first live runs it would sometimes start delegating off the summary it was handed instead of opening the board itself, which defeats the point of having a router. Now ask_teammate refuses for the Boss until get_tickets has actually been called, and it reads the board first on every single ticket. You can see it in the trail: the Boss's own tool list starts with get_tickets every time.

The thing I did not expect, and the most useful thing I learned from running real agents instead of a script, is that the Boss does not make exactly the same call twice. Across runs it sometimes raised the $840 invoice on ticket 101 immediately and sometimes talked itself through the whole blocker and then summarised without flagging the payment. Same ticket, same data, same prompt, different judgement.

That is not something I predicted when I wrote the plan, because a script cannot do it. It is also the reason the system does not trust the agents with the arithmetic. Every ticket is re-checked against its own linked invoice or lease after the team finishes, so an outstanding obligation holds the ticket open and the payment gets raised regardless of what the Boss concluded — it shows up in the trail as Catch missed obligation. The same idea covers amounts: whatever figure an agent proposes is re-read from the database before a human ever sees it. The agents are allowed to vary. The cash is not allowed to vary, and it does not: every run lands on $160.00.

What I would genuinely credit it for is that it mostly stopped doing other people's jobs. On 101 it sent the stock question to Inventory and the customer wording to Customer Service rather than answering both itself, which is what I wanted when I said the Boss should read each ticket and delegate who works on it. Where it is still weak is that its closing summary sometimes repeats what the specialists already said instead of making a call, and it is noticeably better at routing than at being on the receiving end — when Facilities delegated back up to it, the Boss answered in its own structured format rather than the plain sentence a teammate actually wanted. It is built to hand work out, not to take it in.

Inventory — the best performer, and the one that surprised me

Inventory did the single smartest thing in the whole project, and it was not something I predicted. On 101 it checked the shelf, found 0 Classic Bulldog Tees in size S in Aisle B, and then did not stop there. It looked up the vendor, found that Bulldog Print Co holds an unpaid invoice, worked out that the five-day lead time is meaningless until that invoice clears, and handed the money problem straight to Accounting itself rather than reporting a dead end back to the Boss.

That Inventory → Accounting edge is in the audit trail and it is the clearest evidence that the full connectivity is real and not just Boss fan-out. It is also exactly the behaviour you want from a stockroom: the thing blocking the order was never a stock problem, it was an accounts problem wearing a stock problem's clothes, and Inventory figured that out on its own.

Accounting — careful with money, occasionally too careful to be useful

Accounting never once tried to move money, which is the behaviour that actually matters. On both paying tickets it read the live balance, stated what would be left, and stopped. On 101 it reported invoice 501 at $840 and said plainly that it had not made or recorded a payment and that a human had to authorise the release. That is the right instinct and I did not have to prompt it into being cautious.

Where it underperformed is 103. It gave me the margin maths correctly — $22.00 unit cost against $58.00 list, a true margin of 62.07% — and then declined to name a discount, saying the minimum "depends on the margin floor you want to protect." Technically true, but it had a 20% floor available in its own instructions and did not apply it. It handed the decision back to me instead of making the call it was asked to make. An accountant who will not give you a number is not that much more useful than a spreadsheet.

Facilities — correct, and honest about what it could not know

Facilities read lease 1 properly: Chapel Street shop, $2,400 a month, next due 2026-09-02. Then it did something I did not expect and actually respect — it told me it could not confirm how urgent that was, because "the tools available here do not expose Desk.date_today, so I can't confirm today's date."

It was right, and that was a real gap in what we built. Our whole overdue rule depends on desk.date_today and there was no tool exposing it, so the agent either had to guess today's date or refuse to judge. It refused, which is the correct failure. I added a get_desk_today MCP tool because of that, and Facilities now reads the date and states the days remaining. I would rather have an agent that says "I cannot know this" than one that invents a date, and this is the clearest example in the project of an agent catching a design flaw I missed.

Customer Service — right tone, and it never overpromised

Customer Service was the one I was most worried about, because the easy failure mode is an agent that tells the customer whatever makes them happy. It did not do that. Its 101 draft says the tee is out of stock and that we are confirming next steps, and it explicitly does not give Tauhid a delivery date — which is correct, because at that moment the reprint was still blocked behind an unpaid invoice and any date would have been a guess. It also tagged the draft as internal and noted that no customer contact had been made.

Its weakness is that it is the agent most likely to get skipped. It sits at the end of the chain, so when the team is working through a blocker it sometimes never gets asked — which is the main divergence in Question 2.

Question 2 — For each ticket, how did the actual compare to the expected plan?

Worth saying up front: these are live agents choosing their own routing, so the run is not supposed to replay my prediction move for move. A divergence is only a problem if the choice was wrong, not if it was different.

Ticket 101 — matched, and then went further than I planned

I predicted the Boss would call Inventory first because we have an out of stock item, then Accounting for the overdue invoice because that is a payments and billing issue, then Customer Service to draft a message saying the tee is out of stock and getting reprinted. All four agents were involved, exactly as predicted, and everyone but Facilities was called — which is what I said in my plan.

What I did not predict at all is that the run is not the same twice. The agents are live, so on different runs the Boss reached Customer Service first, or went to Inventory first, or looped Accounting in through Inventory rather than directly. The ticket lands in the same place every time and the cash is identical every time, but the path is not fixed the way my plan drew it. That is worth saying plainly: a plan for an agent team is a prediction about a distribution, not about one run.

The other difference is the shape. I drew it as a straight line out of the Boss: Boss to Inventory, Boss to Accounting, Boss to Customer Service. What actually happened is that Inventory reached Accounting directly. My plan had the Boss as a switchboard that every message routes through, and the team found a shorter path that I think is genuinely better, because the agent that discovered the blocker is the one that escalated it, with the context already in hand. If I rewrote the plan now I would draw it that way.

Ticket 102 — matched exactly, and it is the most boring ticket for a reason

I predicted Boss to Facilities first, because they are in charge of the shop space including leases and rent, then to Accounting because it is a cash outflow. That is precisely what happened, with get_lease_details, get_all_leases and get_cash_balance all firing.

I got every prediction right here and I do not think I deserve much credit for it, because rent is structurally obvious — one lease, one landlord, one amount, one date, no hidden second problem. The useful part was watching the human gate hold. We had $2,560 and the rent was $2,400. The agent could have justified paying it automatically: funds sufficient, deadline two days out, rule unambiguous. It did not. It prepared the payment and left it. A box appears on the board saying needs your approval with the amount, the payee and the $160 I would be left with, and the line "only you can release the cash." Paying it automatically would have emptied the account to $160 and nothing would have warned me, because the agent cannot see that I might have another expense planned. That was the exact reason I wanted the human in the loop when I wrote the plan, and it is the one place the design paid off most visibly.

Ticket 103 — routing right, but the evidence-gathering moved around

I predicted Boss to Inventory first for a feasibility check, spotting the 12-hoodie gap, then Accounting for margin, then Customer Service for the discount message, with the margin confirmed healthy before any number goes out. Inventory and Accounting both ran and both got it right: 8 on hand in Aisle A against 20 requested, shortfall 12, and the margin maths correct.

Two things diverged. First, Customer Service is the agent most likely to get skipped on 103. It sits at the end of the chain, so when the team is busy working through the stock gap and the margin, the Boss sometimes closes out before drafting anything for the Yale AI Club. I disagree with that call — the entire point of 103 is that a student org is waiting on a price, so the ticket is not really finished until someone has written what they are told. It is the clearest example of the team optimising for answering the question rather than finishing the job, and it is the behaviour I would target first if I kept iterating on the prompts.

Second, the tool usage moves between runs. I predicted get_open_invoices_for_vendor would fire on 103, and sometimes it does and sometimes it does not, depending on how the Boss delegates. That prediction was the right one to make: the 12 hoodies come from Bulldog Print Co, the same vendor sitting on invoice 501 from ticket 101, so without that check an agent could promise a five-day lead it cannot honour. When Inventory does call it, it correctly reports that the lead time only starts once the invoice clears — and because 101 runs first and pays that invoice, by 103 the same check comes back clean.

That instability is the honest headline finding of this project. With a script, predicting the tools is a test of whether I understood the problem. With live agents, it is partly a test of whether the model felt like being thorough on that particular run. The routing was stable across runs; the evidence-gathering was not. If I were grading my own system, the routing gets a good mark and the consistency does not.

Question 3 — What would have been simpler as one agent with many tools, and why?

Honestly, most of it. Sometimes all the routing can get confusing, and it would be easier if I could click on each specific agent and see its processes instead of a short summary — route ticket for Boss, feasibility check for Inventory. It is readable, but it gets overwhelming as this scales, and with five agents delegating to each other the trail for a single ticket can run to a dozen entries for what is, underneath, three database reads.

Beyond the interface, here is what the live runs actually taught me:

  • The routing barely varies, so most of the machinery is doing nothing. A rent notice goes to Facilities then Accounting. A stock issue starts at Inventory. Across every run I did, the Boss never once made a routing call that could credibly have gone another way. When the routing is effectively a lookup table, five agents are five chances to get wrong a decision that was never in doubt.
  • Handoffs throw away context and then pay to rebuild it. This is the strongest argument and I only saw it once the agents were real. On 101, Inventory looked up the vendor's unpaid invoice, then handed the problem to Accounting — which looked up the same invoice again. Both agents called get_open_invoices_for_vendor for the same fact. One agent holding the ticket calls it once.
  • They talk in circles if you let them. When I raised the delegation depth so Customer Service could be reached, Inventory and Accounting started asking each other the same question back and forth and burned through the request budget agreeing with one another. I had to add a cycle guard so an agent already working on a ticket cannot be asked again. A single agent cannot have a committee meeting with itself.
  • More agents means more places for a step to go unowned. When the Boss summarised 101 without flagging the payment, every individual agent had answered its own piece correctly — nobody owned the question "is this actually finished?" One agent holding the whole ticket has nowhere for that question to fall through.
  • It costs real money and real time. A ticket that resolves to three database reads was taking ten-plus model calls across five agents. The shop rules give us a $1–2 token budget for a reason.
  • Ten tools is not a lot. The usual case for splitting agents is that one prompt cannot hold every tool without getting confused. We have ten, and they are not subtle. The specialisation is buying less than it costs at this scale.

What the split genuinely buys is accountability. Because every step is attributed to a named agent with the tools it called, I can look at the board and see that Accounting read the balance before writing the memo, and that Customer Service read the stock before drafting. One agent doing all of it in a single pass gives me the same answer with far less visibility into whether it checked anything. For a system whose whole job is moving money, that traceability is worth real overhead.

So my conclusion is split: this should be one agent with ten tools and a named-step audit trail, not five agents with a messaging layer. I want the accountability the team gives me without paying for the committee.

And whichever way it is built, the design lesson I would carry forward is that the guardrails belong in the system, not in the prompt. Every protection that held in this project is a check the backend runs after the model has spoken: the amount re-read from the database before a human sees it, the balance checked before a payment is ever offered, the linked invoice re-examined so a ticket cannot close over an unpaid obligation, and the human who has to click approve. That is why the cash lands on exactly $160.00 on every single run even though the agents never take quite the same path twice — the reasoning is allowed to vary because the arithmetic is not relying on it.

Question 4 — Three new problems Campus Customs might face that this agent team could solve with the tools we built

A. Quoting a product we have never carried

Someone proposes carrying a new crewneck and wants to know whether it is worth it. Boss reads the ticket and routes to Inventory, which calls get_vendors to find which supplier actually does apparel reprints and what their lead time is, and get_open_invoices_for_vendor to confirm that supplier is not already blocked — which, as ticket 101 proved, is not a hypothetical. Accounting calls get_pricing on our comparable products to see what the shop already charges and what margin those hold, and get_cash_balance to check whether we can fund an opening order at all. Customer Service drafts the proposal. Every tool this needs already exists; the only new thing is a ticket type.

B. A closure or a supply disruption

There is a snowstorm and the shop is shut for the day, or it is near Christmas and a supplier gets wiped out by a blizzard so orders go onto backlog. Boss routes to Inventory, which uses get_tickets to pull every open order and get_inventory_stock to compare what is on the shelf against what was promised, then get_vendors to see whose lead time just became fiction and get_desk_today to anchor the dates it quotes. Customer Service drafts the notice for the mailing list.

The part that matters here is that drafts stay internal on the board, so a human reads it before it goes out — and that matters far more when the message is going to everyone at once rather than to one person. The same shape covers a holiday promotion: Accounting runs get_pricing first to find a discount that still clears the margin floor, and only then does Customer Service name a number. That ordering is the thing worth keeping — the number gets cleared before it gets written, not after. Ticket 103 is the proof it is needed, because that is the one place the team got the ordering wrong.

C. Working out what is actually selling

We want to know which products move and in which sizes. Inventory runs get_inventory_stock across every SKU and size to find where we are at zero and where we are overstocked — right now that flags the tee in size S and the crest mug both at zero, while size L sits at 14 and the caps sit at 40. Accounting pairs that with get_pricing to rank which are worth restocking first by margin, and get_cash_balance to say what we can afford this week. The output is a restock priority list that Accounting can hand to Inventory, which also tells us how fast the reshipments actually need to come in.

I want to be precise about the limit, because it leads straight into the next question: this tells us what is missing, not what sold. A zero could mean the tee is our best seller or that we only ever ordered one. The database has no sales history, so the agents can rank shortages but they cannot rank demand.

Question 5 — Three new problems this agent team could not solve with the tools we built, and what it would take

A. Scheduling and trading employee shifts

We have no system for this at all. There is no staff table, no availability, no shift calendar. The database knows about products, vendors, leases and cash, and nothing whatsoever about people — which is a strange gap for a shop, since staffing is probably what a student manager actually spends the most time on.

What it would need: a staff and shifts schema; tools like get_shift_schedule, get_staff_availability, propose_shift_swap and confirm_shift_swap; and a Scheduling agent that owns coverage rules — minimum staff per shift, who is trained on the register, who cannot work during midterms. It belongs behind the same human gate we use for cash, because a swap that leaves the shop uncovered on a game day is its own kind of expensive. It would also need get_desk_today, which we now have, since every shift question is a date question.

B. Talking to suppliers, or finding new ones

We currently cannot communicate with suppliers and we cannot discover new ones. The vendors table has exactly three rows that somebody typed in, and the shop rules say do not call vendors — every draft stays internal. So the idea in 4A, where Inventory and Accounting work out what we want made and send it to a producer, stops dead at the point of actually sending it. Worse, if all three vendors were blocked or too slow, the team cannot answer "who else could do this?" — it would just report that we are stuck. Ticket 101 came one unpaid invoice away from exactly that situation.

What it would need: an outbound channel — a send_vendor_rfq tool with a real email or supplier-portal integration — plus a search_supplier_directory tool hitting an external source, and a Procurement agent that can run a quote comparison across replies on lead time and unit cost. Outbound messages need a human approval gate for the same reason payments do: once it leaves the building you cannot take it back.

C. Returns, refunds, and anything that puts money back

Our entire cash model runs one direction. The shop rules say cash only flows out, there is no revenue path anywhere in the database, and both log_payment and POST /approve-payment only ever subtract from checking. So the moment a customer returns a hoodie or disputes a charge, the team has nowhere to put it. There is no returns table, no way to reverse a payment, and no record that a sale ever brought money in.

This quietly limits everything else too. Accounting can calculate that a hoodie at $49.30 returns a 55.4% margin, but it can never check whether the Yale AI Club actually bought them at that price. We can forecast and we can never reconcile, which means the agents are confident about numbers nobody is ever held to.

What it would need: a sales and returns schema so a sale exists as a record in the first place; tools like log_sale, log_refund and get_sales_history; a cash model that moves in both directions; and a Returns agent working with Accounting on restocking fees and refund eligibility. A refund is a payment, so it belongs behind the same approval gate.

One more I would add if I had a fourth slot. Nothing in the system ever learns whether a delivery actually arrived. We can mark invoice 501 paid and state that the reprint lands in five days, and the ticket will read resolved while Tauhid is still waiting for a tee that may never have shipped. It would need a receiving table, a log_receipt tool and carrier tracking. I find this the most uncomfortable gap of the three, because it is the one where the board can be confidently wrong rather than simply silent.
