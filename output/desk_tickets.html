What carried between tickets

Running the three tickets in order left state behind that later tickets picked up, which is the whole reason the board is worth watching in sequence:

  • Invoice 501 opened the gate for 103. Ticket 101 paid Bulldog Print Co, so by the time ticket 103 asked the same vendor for 12 hoodies, Inventory's get_open_invoices_for_vendor check came back clear and the five-day lead time actually held. Run 103 first and the same agent correctly refuses to promise a date.
  • The balance is read live, never cached. Accounting on ticket 102 quoted $2,560.00, not the $3,400.00 it would have seen at the start, because ticket 101's payment had already landed.
  • Stock counts did not move. Nothing shipped during this run, so inventory is unchanged. The tee reprint and the hoodie top-up are both still inbound.

1. How would you evaluate the performance of the agents on each ticket, and why?

Across all three tickets I would say the team performed well, but the three tickets tested genuinely different things, and the agents were not equally impressive on each. What I was really evaluating was not whether they produced an answer — they always produce an answer — but whether they went and checked before they said it.

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee

I predicted the boss would call inventory first because we have an out of stock item, that delegation would go to accounting for the overdue invoice since that is a payment and billing issue, and that there would be a customer service message drafted saying the tee would be back in stock but there are none currently and we are getting them reprinted. The agents matched my prediction exactly, and every tool I expected fired for the agent I expected it to fire for.

What I think they actually did well here is the chain of reasoning, not the routing. Inventory checks the shelf, finds we are one short, and then — this is the part I care about — it does not stop at "we are out, order more." It checks the vendor and finds that Bulldog Print Co is holding an unpaid invoice and will not ship until it clears. That is a second-order problem. A stock shortage looks like an inventory problem, but the thing actually blocking us is an accounting problem, and inventory figured that out and handed it over rather than reporting a dead end.

Accounting then checks the real balance, confirms invoice 501 is $840.00 and three days overdue, and prepares the payment instead of making it. Customer service drafts a reply that says we are out today, a reprint is coming with a five-day lead, and we will confirm a date before charging. I want to flag that last clause specifically, because the easy thing for an agent to do is promise a delivery date to make the customer happy. It refused to, and it said why. That is the single best thing the team did on this ticket.

Ticket 102 — Rent due

I predicted the boss would call facilities first, because they are the ones in charge of the shop space and that includes rents and leases, and we are talking about Elm City Properties. Then I expected delegation to accounting because we are looking at a cash outflow. This was an exact match on both routing and tools, so the agents did a fairly good job here as well.

This was the simplest ticket and in a way the least interesting, because there is no hidden second problem — rent is due, we have the money, pay it. But it is the ticket where the human-in-the-loop design shows up most clearly. We had $2,560.00 in checking and the rent was $2,400.00. The agent could have justified paying it automatically: funds are sufficient, the deadline is two days out, the rule is unambiguous. It did not. It wrote a memo saying funds are sufficient but rent is the largest outflow on the board, so this goes to a human.

That is exactly the reasoning I wanted, and it is the reasoning I gave in my original plan — even though there is enough to pay the rent, a human might be planning another expense that the database does not know about. The agent cannot see my intentions. It can only see $2,560.00. A box appears at the bottom of the board that says needs your approval, shows the amount, who it goes to, and what the balance will be afterward, and says only I can release the cash. Paying it would have left $160.00, and nothing in the system would have warned me that I had just spent almost everything.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount

I predicted the boss would call inventory first to check feasibility and spot the 12-hoodie gap, then accounting for margin, then customer service for the message. The routing matched when I ran it — inventory, then accounting, then customer service — but the tools did not. get_open_invoices_for_vendor never fired, and that is a gate I had to go back and have Claude fix.

I want to be honest that this is the ticket where the agents performed worst, and it is worth explaining why it mattered rather than just noting the miss. The 12 hoodies we are short come from Bulldog Print Co — the same vendor sitting on the unpaid invoice from ticket 101. Without that check, inventory was willing to tell customer service that the gap closes in five days, and customer service would have put that in writing to the Yale AI Club. The routing was perfect and the answer was still wrong, because the agent did not verify a fact it was asserting.

That is the lesson I took from this whole assignment. Predicting which agent gets called is the easy half. The hard half is predicting which tools they call, because that is where the difference between a confident answer and a true one lives. After the fix, inventory reports that the five-day lead only starts once invoice 501 clears. And because 101 runs first in a full run and pays that invoice, by the time 103 runs the same check comes back clean and the lead time genuinely holds. The agent now tells the truth in both directions, and it only does that because the tool fires.

One thing I found by testing rather than running

Running the three tickets as written only proves the agents work on the data we happen to have. We changed the hoodie's unit cost in the database from $22.00 to $40.00 and ran 103 again, and accounting said the 20% margin floor put the lowest workable price at $50.00 and then recommended $49.30 anyway. It stated a rule and immediately broke it, because the 15% discount was hardcoded instead of being checked against the floor.

That bug was invisible at our real prices, where a 15% discount leaves a 55.4% margin and the floor never binds. It is fixed now — the discount is clamped to the floor, and if costs rise so far that even list price cannot clear 20%, accounting refuses to quote anything and escalates to a human while customer service withholds the draft entirely. I am including this because I think it is the most useful thing I learned: an agent that produces a reasonable answer on your sample data has not been tested, it has been demonstrated.

2. For each ticket, how did the actual compare to the expected plan?

Ticket 101 — full match. Our actual was perfectly aligned with our expected plan. It matched my first-call guess of inventory, the agents involved were exactly the four I named (everyone but facilities), and all of the tools I predicted fired. log_payment fired through the approval route rather than being called by an agent directly, which is the correct behaviour and still counts as predicted.

Ticket 102 — full match. Boss to facilities to accounting, exactly as planned, with get_tickets, get_lease_details, get_all_leases and get_cash_balance all firing. This is the one ticket where I got every single prediction right, and I think that is because rent is the most structurally obvious ticket on the board — there is one lease, one landlord, one amount, one date.

Ticket 103 — routing matched, tools did not. The agent sequence was exactly what I predicted. The gap was get_open_invoices_for_vendor, which I had listed in my plan and which never fired until we fixed it. I count this as my prediction being right and the implementation being wrong, which is a more interesting outcome than a clean match — it means the plan was a useful check on the build rather than just a description of it.

The broader pattern across all three: my routing predictions were three for three, and my tool predictions were two for three. Deciding who handles a rent notice is not actually hard, and I do not think I deserve much credit for getting it right. Deciding what evidence they need to gather before they answer is where the real design work is, and it is also where the one failure was.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why?

Honestly, most of it. Sometimes all the routing can get confusing, and it would be easier if I could click on each specific agent to see its processes. Right now I am mostly getting a summary — route ticket for boss, feasibility check for inventory — which is readable, but it can get overwhelming or confusing, and I think that gets worse as this scales.

Beyond the interface, there are a few concrete reasons one agent with many tools would have been simpler for a shop this size:

  • The routing never actually varies. A rent notice always goes to facilities then accounting. A stock issue always starts at inventory. Across all three tickets the boss never once made a judgment call that could have gone another way. When the routing is effectively a lookup table, five agents are five opportunities to hand off wrong for a decision that was never in doubt.
  • Every handoff throws away context. Inventory already discovered that the vendor is blocked by an unpaid invoice, but it cannot act on that — it has to re-state it in a sentence and pass it to accounting, which then looks up the same invoice again. One agent holding the whole ticket would have called get_open_invoices_for_vendor once and moved on.
  • Nine tools is not many. The usual argument for splitting agents is that one prompt cannot hold every tool without getting confused. We have nine, and they are not subtle. The specialisation is buying us less than it costs at this scale.
  • Cost and latency. Five agent turns per ticket instead of one is five times the tokens for a ticket whose entire resolution is three database reads and a payment.

Where the team genuinely earns its keep is the audit trail. Because every step is attributed to a named agent with the tools it called, I can look at the board and see that accounting checked the balance before writing the memo, and that customer service read pricing before quoting a number. If one agent did all of that in a single pass, I would get the same answer with far less visibility into whether it actually verified anything. For a system whose whole job is moving money, that traceability is worth real overhead.

So my honest conclusion is that the split is overkill as a routing mechanism and valuable as an accountability mechanism, and if I rebuilt this I would keep the five names as labels on the audit trail while letting far fewer actual agents do the work.

4. Three new problems Campus Customs might face that this agent team could solve with the tools we built

A. Quoting a product we have never carried

Someone proposes carrying a new crewneck and wants to know whether it is worth it. Boss routes to inventory, which calls get_vendors to find which supplier actually does apparel reprints and what their lead time is, and get_open_invoices_for_vendor to confirm that supplier is not already blocked. Accounting calls get_pricing on our comparable products to see what the shop already charges and what margin those hold, and get_cash_balance to check whether we can even fund an opening order. Customer service drafts the proposal. Every tool this needs already exists — the only new thing is a ticket type.

B. A closure or a supply disruption

There is a snowstorm and the shop is closed for the day, or it is near Christmas and a supplier gets wiped out by a blizzard so orders go onto backlog. Boss routes to inventory, which uses get_tickets to pull every open order and get_inventory_stock to compare what is actually on the shelf against what was promised, then get_vendors to see whose lead time just became fiction. Customer service drafts the notice for the mailing list, and because drafts stay internal on the board, a human reads it before anything goes out — which matters a lot more when the message is going to everyone at once instead of one person.

The same shape covers a holiday promotion. Accounting runs get_pricing first to find a discount that still clears the margin floor, and only then does customer service name a number. That ordering is the thing worth keeping: the number gets cleared before it gets written, not after.

C. Working out what is actually selling

We want to know which products move and in which sizes. Inventory runs get_inventory_stock across every SKU and size to find where we are at zero and where we are overstocked — right now that flags the tee in size S and the crest mug both at zero, while size L sits at 14 and the caps sit at 40. Accounting pairs that with get_pricing to rank which of those are worth restocking first by margin, and get_cash_balance to say what we can actually afford this week. The output is a restock priority list.

I want to be precise about the limit here, though, because it bleeds into the next question: this tells us what is missing, not what sold. A zero could mean the tee is our best seller or that we only ever ordered one. The database has no sales history, so the agents can rank shortages but they cannot rank demand.

5. Three new problems this agent team could not solve with the tools we built

A. Scheduling and trading employee shifts

We have no system for this at all. There is no staff table, no availability, no shift calendar. The database knows about products, vendors, leases and cash, and nothing whatsoever about people — which is a strange gap for a shop, since staffing is probably the thing a student manager actually spends the most time on.

What it would need: a staff and shifts schema; tools like get_shift_schedule, get_staff_availability, propose_shift_swap and confirm_shift_swap; and a Scheduling agent that owns coverage rules — minimum staff per shift, who is trained on the register, who cannot work during midterms. It should sit behind the same human approval gate we use for cash, because a swap that leaves the shop uncovered on a game day is its own kind of expensive.

B. Talking to suppliers, or finding new ones

We currently cannot communicate with suppliers directly and we cannot discover new ones. The vendors table has exactly three rows that somebody typed in, and the shop rules explicitly say do not call vendors — every draft stays internal on the board. So the idea in 4A, where inventory and accounting work out what we want made and send it to a producer, stops dead at the point of actually sending it. Worse, if all three of our vendors were blocked or too slow, the team has no way to answer "who else could do this?" — it would just report that we are stuck.

What it would need: an outbound channel — a send_vendor_rfq tool with a real email or supplier-portal integration — plus a search_supplier_directory tool hitting an external source, and a Procurement agent that can run a quote comparison across replies on lead time and unit cost. Outbound messages need a human approval gate for the same reason payments do: once it leaves the building you cannot take it back.

C. Returns, refunds, and anything that puts money back

Our entire cash model runs one direction. The shop rules say cash only flows out, there is no revenue path anywhere in the database, and both log_payment and POST /approve-payment only ever subtract from checking. So the moment a customer returns a hoodie or disputes a charge, the team has nowhere to put it. There is no returns table, no way to reverse a payment, and no record that a sale ever brought money in at all.

This also quietly limits everything else. Accounting can calculate that a hoodie at $49.30 returns a 55.4% margin, but it can never check whether the Yale AI Club actually bought them at that price. We can forecast but we can never reconcile, which means the agents are confident about numbers they have no way to be accountable for.

What it would need: a sales and returns schema so a sale exists as a record in the first place; tools like log_sale, log_refund and get_sales_history; a cash model that moves in both directions; and a Returns agent working with accounting on restocking fees and refund eligibility. A refund is a payment, so it belongs behind the same approval gate.

Two alternates for C, if we would rather swap it out. (1) Confirming a delivery actually arrived. The board can mark invoice 501 paid and state that the reprint lands in five days, but nothing in the system ever learns whether the box showed up — so a ticket can read "resolved" while the customer is still waiting. It would need a receiving table, a log_receipt tool and carrier tracking. (2) Anything with a legal or tax consequence. Facilities tracks rent and due dates but has no view of lease renewal terms, notice periods, or sales tax on the orders we quote, and those are exactly the mistakes that are expensive and irreversible.
